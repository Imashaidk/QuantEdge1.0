\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Most banks and asset managers scale daily Value-at-Risk (VaR) to multi-day horizons using the standard Basel square-root-of-time rule ($\text{VaR}_h = \text{VaR}_1 \times \sqrt{h}$). This rule assumes that asset co-movements during extreme events stay identical whether positions are held for an afternoon or for three months. In this paper, we directly answer the official SAIFA Quant Edge 1.0 research question: \textit{``Does tail dependence change with the investment horizon, and what does ignoring this do to a portfolio's measured risk?''} Using daily data from 2015 to 2026 across a five-pillar institutional universe (US Equities, Tech, Long Treasuries, Gold, and High-Yield Credit), we decompose returns across timescales using the Maximal Overlap Discrete Wavelet Transform (MODWT). After filtering out volatility clustering with AR(1)-GJR-GARCH(1,1) models and modeling fat tails using Extreme Value Theory (EVT-POT), we run an automated tournament across five copula families at every timescale.

Our empirical findings demonstrate that cross-asset tail crash dependence persists across investment horizons. Lower tail crash dependence is $\hat{\lambda}_L = 0.189$ at daily noise scales ($D_1$, 2 to 4 days) and $\hat{\lambda}_L = 0.201$ at weekly swing scales ($D_2$, 4 to 8 days), stabilizing at $0.052$ at quarterly horizons ($D_5$). Across all scales, the Student-$t$ copula decisively wins the model tournament by Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), demonstrating that multi-asset joint tail risk is governed by elliptical fat tails rather than asymmetric Archimedean structures. Over 875 out-of-sample trading days (2023 to 2026), conventional square-root-of-time scaling was statistically conservative at 5-day and 20-day horizons (0 to 3 breaches vs $\approx 8.7$ expected), while the 1-day Parametric Gaussian model generates 13 breaches (a $1.49\%$ failure rate), demonstrating that assuming normality understates short-term tail risk. We introduce the \textbf{Horizon-Conditioned Tail Capital Multiplier (H-TCM)} as a closed-form contingent policy overlay that dynamically adds a targeted capital buffer ($+2.2\%$ at weekly horizons) only when horizon tail dependence exceeds the daily baseline, avoiding unnecessary capital drag during quiet markets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction \& Research Question}
\label{sec:intro}

In quantitative risk management, Value-at-Risk ($\text{VaR}_\alpha$) and Expected Shortfall ($\text{ES}_\alpha$) form the core metrics used to determine regulatory capital buffers under the Basel framework \cite{basel1996, mcneil2015}. Under Basel guidelines, banks calculate their 1-day or 10-day risk numbers. When trading desks manage illiquid assets or hold positions across weeks, they almost universally rely on the classic square-root-of-time rule:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:basel_sqrt}
\text{VaR}_h = \text{VaR}_1 \times \sqrt{h}
\end{equation}
This formula was popularized decades ago as a computational shortcut. Mathematically, it requires two strict assumptions: asset returns must be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) normal random variables, and the joint correlation structure between assets must remain completely unchanged across all holding periods.

In real financial markets, both assumptions fall apart:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single-Asset Margins Have Fat Tails:} Returns cluster in volatile bursts, react more violently to bad news than good news (the leverage effect), and produce extreme losses far more often than a bell curve predicts.
    \item \textbf{Joint Tail Dependence Changes with the Horizon:} High-frequency day traders, weekly swing traders, and quarterly pension rebalancers operate on completely different clocks \cite{gencay2001, percival2000}. Two assets that move independently over an afternoon can become heavily tied together over a multi-week horizon.
\end{enumerate}

This study directly resolves the central competition inquiry:
\begin{quote}
\textit{``Does tail dependence change with the investment horizon, and what does ignoring this do to a portfolio's measured risk?''}
\end{quote}

Our core contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We show why standard decimated wavelets (DWT) fail in empirical finance by discarding observations, and use the shift-invariant \textbf{Maximal Overlap Discrete Wavelet Transform (MODWT)} to keep all $N = 2,889$ observations across every decomposition level.
    \item We filter out volatility clustering with an $\text{AR}(1)\text{-GJR-GARCH}(1,1)$ model and fit Generalized Pareto Distributions ($\text{GPD}$) to the extreme $10\%$ tails, ensuring pure uniform margins before copula estimation.
    \item We run an automated tournament across five copula families (Gaussian, Student-$t$, Clayton, Gumbel, Frank) across six timescale bands, demonstrating that fat-tailed Student-$t$ co-dependence dominates across holding periods while empirical tail crash dependence remains persistent.
    \item We demonstrate through 875 days of out-of-sample backtesting (2023 to 2026) the performance of benchmark and multiscale risk estimators, and deliver the \textbf{Horizon-Conditioned Tail Capital Multiplier (H-TCM)} as an actionable contingent overlay for institutional desks.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Asset Universe \& Economic Intuition}
\label{sec:universe}

Many empirical studies examine pairs of tech stocks (such as Apple and Microsoft). While easy to model, these pairs provide little insight into portfolio risk because tech stocks share high positive beta across all horizons. To examine whether cross-asset diversification holds up under stress, we analyze a five-pillar institutional portfolio from January 2, 2015 to June 30, 2026 ($N = 2,889$ trading days):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SPY} ($30\%$): S\&P 500 ETF (Core US equity market).
    \item \textbf{QQQ} ($20\%$): Invesco QQQ Trust (High-beta growth and technology).
    \item \textbf{TLT} ($25\%$): 20+ Year Treasury Bond ETF (Duration hedge and flight to safety).
    \item \textbf{GLD} ($15\%$): SPDR Gold Shares (Inflation hedge and alternative store of value).
    \item \textbf{HYG} ($10\%$): iShares High Yield Corporate Bond ETF (Credit spread and liquidity risk).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cross-Asset Diversification Across Timescales}
On an ordinary trading day ($D_1$: 2 to 4 days), Treasuries (TLT) and Gold (GLD) serve as effective hedges against stock market drops. When equities decline slightly, investors buy bonds or gold, keeping linear correlation low or negative. This is the foundation of the traditional balanced portfolio.

However, during market shocks, liquidity demands can alter cross-asset dynamics. Leveraged desks face margin calls on losing equity and credit positions and may liquidate liquid holdings (Treasuries and Gold) to meet liquidity calls. Examining a diversified portfolio across scales reveals whether cross-asset tail dependence shifts from high-frequency noise bands to quarterly macroeconomic cycles.

\section{Multiscale Wavelet-Copula Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{MODWT Wavelet Filter Bank}
The standard Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) downsamples the series by a factor of two at each octave ($N \to N/2 \to N/4$). By scale $J=5$, only $N/32$ data points remain (less than 90 observations from a 2,889-day history). Fitting Extreme Value Theory tails or copulas on 90 points is statistically unreliable.

To preserve full statistical power, we use the \textbf{Maximal Overlap Discrete Wavelet Transform (MODWT)} \cite{percival2000}. MODWT is shift-invariant and preserves the complete sample length $N$ at every octave. We select the Symlet filter bank ($\text{sym8}$, filter length $L=16$) because it provides near-linear phase and minimizes edge distortions. The high-pass filter $\tilde{h}$ and low-pass scaling filter $\tilde{g}$ are rescaled by $1/\sqrt{2}$:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{h}_l = \frac{h_l}{\sqrt{2}}, \quad \tilde{g}_l = \frac{g_l}{\sqrt{2}}
\end{equation}
At scale $j \in \{1, \dots, J\}$, filter steps are dilated by $2^{j-1}$. Wavelet detail coefficients $\widetilde{W}_j$ and scaling approximation coefficients $\widetilde{V}_j$ are calculated via circular convolution:
\begin{align}
\widetilde{W}_j[t] &= \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} \tilde{h}_l \widetilde{V}_{j-1}[(t - 2^{j-1} l) \bmod N] \\
\widetilde{V}_j[t] &= \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} \tilde{g}_l \widetilde{V}_{j-1}[(t - 2^{j-1} l) \bmod N]
\end{align}
where $\widetilde{V}_0 = R_t$ represents the raw daily log return series.

\subsection{Additive Multiresolution Analysis (MRA)}
Through inverse pyramid filtering, MODWT provides an exact additive multiresolution reconstruction:
\begin{equation}
R_t = \sum_{j=1}^J D_{j,t} + S_{J,t}
\end{equation}
Here $D_j$ represents scale-specific detail fluctuations and $S_J$ captures the smooth macroeconomic trend. In our data pipeline, we verify that this decomposition reconstructs original returns with machine precision:
\begin{equation}
\max_{t} \left| R_t - \left( \sum_{j=1}^5 D_{j,t} + S_{5,t} \right) \right| = 3.77 \times 10^{-14} \ll 10^{-10}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Semi-Parametric GARCH-EVT Margins}
Fitting copulas directly on raw returns violates Sklar's theorem because financial returns exhibit volatility clustering \cite{mcneil2015}. A cluster of volatile days can easily be mistaken for structural tail dependence when it is simply a shared volatility spike.

We solve this using a two-stage semi-parametric filter:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Volatility Filtering:} For each asset detail series $D_j$, we fit an $\text{AR}(1)\text{-GJR-GARCH}(1,1)$ model with Student-$t$ innovations \cite{gjr1993}:
    \begin{align}
    r_t &= \mu + \phi r_{t-1} + \epsilon_t, \quad \epsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t \\
    \sigma_t^2 &= \omega + (\alpha + \gamma I_{\{\epsilon_{t-1} < 0\}}) \epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2
    \end{align}
    where the parameter $\gamma$ captures the leverage effect (bad news generating higher volatility than good news).
    \item \textbf{EVT Peaks-Over-Threshold (POT):} Standardized filtered residuals $z_t = \epsilon_t / \sigma_t$ are modeled using the empirical cumulative distribution function (CDF) for the central body ($10\% \le u \le 90\%$) and Generalized Pareto Distributions ($\text{GPD}$) \cite{pickands1975} for the extreme $10\%$ tails:
    \begin{equation}
    F(z) = \begin{cases}
    q_L \left( 1 + \xi_L \frac{u_L - z}{\beta_L} \right)^{-1/\xi_L}, & z < u_L \\
    F_{\text{emp}}(z), & u_L \le z \le u_U \\
    1 - (1 - q_U) \left( 1 + \xi_U \frac{z - u_U}{\beta_U} \right)^{-1/\xi_U}, & z > u_U
    \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Probability Integral Transform (PIT):} Transformed margins $U_i = F_i(z_i)$ are verified as independent $\text{Uniform}(0, 1)$ random variables via empirical quantile checks ($\approx 5.0\%$ in the bottom $5\%$ tail).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scale-Optimal Copula Tournament}
According to Sklar's Theorem \cite{sklar1959, embrechts2002}, any multivariate distribution $F(x_1, \dots, x_d)$ decomposes uniquely into its marginal CDFs and a copula function $C$:
\begin{equation}
F(x_1, \dots, x_d) = C(F_1(x_1), \dots, F_d(x_d))
\end{equation}
At each scale $D_1$ through $D_5$ and $S_5$, we run an automated tournament across five copula families fitted via Maximum Likelihood Estimation:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gaussian Copula:} Zero tail dependence benchmark ($\lambda_L = \lambda_U = 0$).
    \item \textbf{Student-$t$ Copula:} Symmetric tail dependence:
    \begin{equation}
    \lambda_L = \lambda_U = 2 t_{\nu + 1}\left( -\sqrt{\frac{(\nu + 1)(1 - \rho)}{1 + \rho}} \right) > 0
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Clayton Copula:} Asymmetric lower crash dependence ($\lambda_L = 2^{-1/\theta} > 0, \lambda_U = 0$).
    \item \textbf{Gumbel Copula:} Asymmetric upper-tail dependence ($\lambda_U = 2 - 2^{1/\theta} > 0, \lambda_L = 0$).
    \item \textbf{Frank Copula:} Radial symmetry with zero tail dependence ($\lambda_L = \lambda_U = 0$).
\end{enumerate}
For Archimedean copula families in dimensions $d > 2$, parameters are estimated via pairwise composite log-likelihood maximization, a standard and computationally robust approach for high-dimensional elliptical and Archimedean comparisons \cite{mcneil2015}. The winning copula per timescale is selected by minimizing the Bayesian Information Criterion:
\begin{equation}
\text{BIC} = k \ln(N) - 2 \ln(\hat{L})
\end{equation}

\subsection{Supervisory Hypothesis Testing}
We evaluate model performance through four established supervisory criteria:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Kupiec POF Likelihood Ratio Test:} Tests whether the observed breach count $x$ over $N$ observations matches the expected nominal rate ($H_0: p = 0.01$) \cite{kupiec1995}:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{LR}_{\text{POF}} = -2 \ln \left[ \frac{(1-p_0)^{N-x} p_0^x}{(1 - \hat{p})^{N-x} \hat{p}^x} \right] \sim \chi^2(1)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Christoffersen Independence Test:} Tests whether breaches cluster together in consecutive time windows \cite{christoffersen1998}:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{LR}_{\text{ind}} = -2 \ln \left[ \frac{L(\pi)}{L(\pi_{01}, \pi_{11})} \right] \sim \chi^2(1)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Basel Committee Traffic Light Zones:} Evaluates 250-day scaled breach counts at $99\%$ VaR:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Green Zone} ($\le 4$ breaches): Approved model ($k = 3.0$).
        \item \textbf{Yellow Zone} (5 to 9 breaches): Capital penalty surcharge ($k \in [3.40, 3.85]$).
        \item \textbf{Red Zone} ($\ge 10$ breaches): Model rejected ($k = 4.0$).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fissler-Ziegel (FZ) Strictly Consistent Loss:} Joint zero-homogeneous scoring function for $(\text{VaR}_{99\%}, \text{ES}_{99\%})$ \cite{fissler2016}:
    \begin{equation}
    S(v, e, y) = \frac{1}{e} \left[ v + \frac{(y - v) I_{\{y > v\}}}{1 - \alpha} \right] + \ln(e) - 1
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Actionable Policy: The H-TCM Multiplier}
\label{sec:htcm}

The competition mandates: \textit{``Give one concrete recommendation a risk manager could act on tomorrow.''}

\subsection{The Closed-Form Formula}
We introduce the \textbf{Horizon-Conditioned Tail Capital Multiplier (H-TCM)}, a practical formula that can be inserted directly into existing risk management software:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:htcm}
\boxed{
\text{VaR}_h^* = \text{VaR}_1 \times \sqrt{h} \times \left[ 1 + \kappa \cdot \max\left(0, \frac{\hat{\lambda}_L(h) - \hat{\lambda}_L(1)}{\hat{\lambda}_L(1) + \epsilon}\right) \right]
}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\text{VaR}_1$: 1-day baseline VaR estimate.
    \item $\sqrt{h}$: Standard square-root-of-time factor.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This research provides a direct answer to the competition challenge: \textbf{Tail dependence persists across investment horizons}. In our five-asset portfolio, lower tail crash dependence remains non-zero across all holding periods ($\hat{\lambda}_L \in [0.052, 0.201]$), and the Student-$t$ copula is selected as optimal across all timescale bands. Ignoring fat tails causes traditional Gaussian models to understate short-term risk (13 breaches at the 1-day horizon). In out-of-sample multi-day backtesting, conventional square-root scaling was conservative, and the H-TCM contingent multiplier provides a targeted mechanism to guard against horizon-specific tail dependence spikes without imposing unnecessary capital drag.
