\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Most banks and asset managers scale daily Value-at-Risk (VaR) to multi-day horizons using the standard Basel square-root-of-time rule ($\text{VaR}_h = \text{VaR}_1 \times \sqrt{h}$). This rule assumes that the way assets move together during extreme events stays identical whether you hold them for an afternoon or for three months. In this paper, we directly answer the official SAIFA Quant Edge 1.0 research question: \textit{``Does tail dependence change with the investment horizon, and what does ignoring this do to a portfolio's measured risk?''} Using daily data from 2015 to 2026 across a five-pillar institutional universe (US Equities, Tech, Long Treasuries, Gold, and High-Yield Credit), we decompose returns across timescales using the Maximal Overlap Discrete Wavelet Transform (MODWT). After filtering out volatility clustering with ARMA-GJR-GARCH models and modeling fat tails using Extreme Value Theory (EVT-POT), we run an automated tournament across five copula families at every timescale.

Our empirical findings reveal what we term the \textbf{Flight-to-Liquidity Contagion Paradox}. Lower tail crash dependence ($\lambda_L$) is very low at high-frequency daily noise scales ($0.042$ at scale $D_1$, 2 to 4 days), but jumps by over $650\%$ to $0.318$ at quarterly business-cycle horizons ($D_5$, 32 to 64 days). Over the same span, the Timescale Asymmetry Ratio ($\text{TAR} = \lambda_L - \lambda_U$) climbs from $0.000$ to $+0.286$. In simple terms: assets that normally hedge each other during calm periods crash together during extended sell-offs because investors dump their most liquid holdings (Treasuries and Gold) to meet cash margin calls. In out-of-sample backtesting across 875 trading days (2023 to 2026), conventional Gaussian models suffer more than double the permitted violations. To give risk managers an immediate fix, we introduce the \textbf{Horizon-Conditioned Tail Capital Multiplier (H-TCM)}, a closed-form formula that dynamically expands capital reserves for multi-day horizons without requiring firms to rebuild their legacy risk systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction \& Research Question}
\label{sec:intro}

In quantitative risk management, Value-at-Risk ($\text{VaR}_\alpha$) and Expected Shortfall ($\text{ES}_\alpha$) form the core metrics used to determine regulatory capital buffers under the Basel framework \cite{basel1996, mcneil2015}. Under Basel guidelines, banks calculate their 1-day or 10-day risk numbers. When trading desks manage illiquid assets or hold positions across weeks, they almost universally rely on the classic square-root-of-time rule:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:basel_sqrt}
\text{VaR}_h = \text{VaR}_1 \times \sqrt{h}
\end{equation}
This formula was popularized decades ago as a computational shortcut. Mathematically, it requires two strict assumptions: asset returns must be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) normal random variables, and the joint correlation structure between assets must remain completely unchanged across all holding periods.

In real financial markets, both assumptions fall apart:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single-Asset Margins Have Fat Tails:} Returns cluster in volatile bursts, react more violently to bad news than good news (the leverage effect), and produce extreme losses far more often than a bell curve predicts.
    \item \textbf{Joint Tail Dependence Changes with the Horizon:} High-frequency day traders, weekly swing traders, and quarterly pension rebalancers operate on completely different clocks \cite{gencay2001, percival2000}. Two assets that move independently over an afternoon can become heavily tied together over a two-month panic.
\end{enumerate}

This study directly resolves the central competition inquiry:
\begin{quote}
\textit{``Does tail dependence change with the investment horizon, and what does ignoring this do to a portfolio's measured risk?''}
\end{quote}

Our core contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We show why standard decimated wavelets (DWT) fail in empirical finance by discarding observations, and use the shift-invariant \textbf{Maximal Overlap Discrete Wavelet Transform (MODWT)} to keep all $N = 2,889$ observations across every decomposition level.
    \item We filter out volatility clustering with an $\text{ARMA}(1,1)\text{-GJR-GARCH}(1,1)$ model and fit Generalized Pareto Distributions ($\text{GPD}$) to the extreme $10\%$ tails, ensuring pure uniform margins before copula estimation.
    \item We run an automated tournament across five copula families (Gaussian, Student-$t$, Clayton, Gumbel, Frank) across six timescale bands, proving that crash dependence surges dramatically at longer holding periods.
    \item We demonstrate through 875 days of out-of-sample backtesting (2023 to 2026) that naive risk models severely undercount multi-day risk, and deliver the \textbf{Horizon-Conditioned Tail Capital Multiplier (H-TCM)} as an actionable plug-and-play formula for institutional desks.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Asset Universe \& Economic Intuition}
\label{sec:universe}

Many empirical studies examine pairs of tech stocks (such as Apple and Microsoft). While easy to model, these pairs provide little insight into portfolio risk because tech stocks share high positive beta across all horizons. To examine whether cross-asset diversification holds up under stress, we analyze a five-pillar institutional portfolio from January 2, 2015 to June 30, 2026 ($N = 2,889$ trading days):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SPY} ($30\%$): S\&P 500 ETF (Core US equity market).
    \item \textbf{QQQ} ($20\%$): Invesco QQQ Trust (High-beta growth and technology).
    \item \textbf{TLT} ($25\%$): 20+ Year Treasury Bond ETF (Duration hedge and flight to safety).
    \item \textbf{GLD} ($15\%$): SPDR Gold Shares (Inflation hedge and alternative store of value).
    \item \textbf{HYG} ($10\%$): iShares High Yield Corporate Bond ETF (Credit spread and liquidity risk).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Flight-to-Liquidity Contagion Paradox}
On an ordinary trading day ($D_1$: 2 to 4 days), Treasuries (TLT) and Gold (GLD) serve as effective hedges against stock market drops. When equities decline slightly, investors buy bonds or gold, keeping linear correlation low or negative. This is the foundation of the traditional 60/40 balanced portfolio.

However, during a severe market shock (such as the March 2020 liquidity freeze), an entirely different mechanism takes over. Leveraged funds face immediate margin calls on their losing equity and credit positions. Because they cannot quickly sell illiquid securities without taking massive losses, they are forced to sell whatever liquid assets they can dump immediately: US Treasuries and Gold. When all institutional desks rush to liquidate safe assets at the same time, the diversification hedge disappears. Over intermediate and quarterly holding periods ($D_4$ to $D_5$: 16 to 64 days), \textbf{lower tail crash dependence surges, leaving portfolios unprotected at the exact moment protection is needed most}.

\section{Multiscale Wavelet-Copula Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{MODWT Wavelet Filter Bank}
The standard Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) downsamples the series by a factor of two at each octave ($N \to N/2 \to N/4$). By scale $J=5$, only $N/32$ data points remain (less than 90 observations from a 2,889-day history). Fitting Extreme Value Theory tails or copulas on 90 points is statistically unreliable.

To preserve full statistical power, we use the \textbf{Maximal Overlap Discrete Wavelet Transform (MODWT)} \cite{percival2000}. MODWT is shift-invariant and preserves the complete sample length $N$ at every octave. We select the Symlet filter bank ($\text{sym8}$, filter length $L=16$) because it provides near-linear phase and minimizes edge distortions. The high-pass filter $\tilde{h}$ and low-pass scaling filter $\tilde{g}$ are rescaled by $1/\sqrt{2}$:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{h}_l = \frac{h_l}{\sqrt{2}}, \quad \tilde{g}_l = \frac{g_l}{\sqrt{2}}
\end{equation}
At scale $j \in \{1, \dots, J\}$, filter steps are dilated by $2^{j-1}$. Wavelet detail coefficients $\widetilde{W}_j$ and scaling approximation coefficients $\widetilde{V}_j$ are calculated via circular convolution:
\begin{align}
\widetilde{W}_j[t] &= \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} \tilde{h}_l \widetilde{V}_{j-1}[(t - 2^{j-1} l) \bmod N] \\
\widetilde{V}_j[t] &= \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} \tilde{g}_l \widetilde{V}_{j-1}[(t - 2^{j-1} l) \bmod N]
\end{align}
where $\widetilde{V}_0 = R_t$ represents the raw daily log return series.

\subsection{Additive Multiresolution Analysis (MRA)}
Through inverse pyramid filtering, MODWT provides an exact additive multiresolution reconstruction:
\begin{equation}
R_t = \sum_{j=1}^J D_{j,t} + S_{J,t}
\end{equation}
Here $D_j$ represents scale-specific detail fluctuations and $S_J$ captures the smooth macroeconomic trend. In our data pipeline, we verify that this decomposition reconstructs original returns with machine precision:
\begin{equation}
\max_{t} \left| R_t - \left( \sum_{j=1}^5 D_{j,t} + S_{5,t} \right) \right| = 3.77 \times 10^{-14} \ll 10^{-10}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Semi-Parametric GARCH-EVT Margins}
Fitting copulas directly on raw returns violates Sklar's theorem because financial returns exhibit volatility clustering \cite{mcneil2015}. A cluster of volatile days can easily be mistaken for structural tail dependence when it is simply a shared volatility spike.

We solve this using a two-stage semi-parametric filter:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Volatility Filtering:} For each asset detail series $D_j$, we fit an $\text{AR}(1)\text{-GJR-GARCH}(1,1)$ model with Student-$t$ innovations \cite{gjr1993}:
    \begin{align}
    r_t &= \mu + \phi r_{t-1} + \epsilon_t, \quad \epsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t \\
    \sigma_t^2 &= \omega + (\alpha + \gamma I_{\{\epsilon_{t-1} < 0\}}) \epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2
    \end{align}
    where the parameter $\gamma$ captures the leverage effect (bad news generating higher volatility than good news).
    \item \textbf{EVT Peaks-Over-Threshold (POT):} Standardized residuals $z_t = \epsilon_t / \sigma_t$ are modeled using the empirical cumulative distribution function (CDF) for the central body ($10\% \le u \le 90\%$) and Generalized Pareto Distributions ($\text{GPD}$) \cite{pickands1975} for the extreme $10\%$ tails:
    \begin{equation}
    F(z) = \begin{cases}
    q_L \left( 1 + \xi_L \frac{u_L - z}{\beta_L} \right)^{-1/\xi_L}, & z < u_L \\
    F_{\text{emp}}(z), & u_L \le z \le u_U \\
    1 - (1 - q_U) \left( 1 + \xi_U \frac{z - u_U}{\beta_U} \right)^{-1/\xi_U}, & z > u_U
    \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Probability Integral Transform (PIT):} Transformed margins $U_i = F_i(z_i)$ are verified as independent $\text{Uniform}(0, 1)$ random variables via Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests ($p > 0.05$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scale-Optimal Copula Tournament}
According to Sklar's Theorem \cite{sklar1959, embrechts2002}, any multivariate distribution $F(x_1, \dots, x_d)$ decomposes uniquely into its marginal CDFs and a copula function $C$:
\begin{equation}
F(x_1, \dots, x_d) = C(F_1(x_1), \dots, F_d(x_d))
\end{equation}
At each scale $D_1$ through $D_5$ and $S_5$, we run an automated tournament across five copula families fitted via Maximum Likelihood Estimation:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gaussian Copula:} Zero tail dependence benchmark ($\lambda_L = \lambda_U = 0$).
    \item \textbf{Student-$t$ Copula:} Symmetric tail dependence:
    \begin{equation}
    \lambda_L = \lambda_U = 2 t_{\nu + 1}\left( -\sqrt{\frac{(\nu + 1)(1 - \rho)}{1 + \rho}} \right) > 0
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Clayton Copula:} Asymmetric lower crash dependence:
    \begin{equation}
    \lambda_L = 2^{-1/\theta} > 0, \quad \lambda_U = 0
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Gumbel Copula:} Asymmetric upper-tail dependence (and lower tail when inverted):
    \begin{equation}
    \lambda_U = 2 - 2^{1/\theta} > 0
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Frank Copula:} Radial symmetry with zero tail dependence ($\lambda_L = \lambda_U = 0$).
\end{enumerate}
For Archimedean copula families in dimensions $d > 2$, parameters are estimated via pairwise composite log-likelihood maximization, a standard and computationally robust approach for high-dimensional elliptical and Archimedean comparisons \cite{mcneil2015}. The winning copula per timescale is selected by minimizing the Bayesian Information Criterion:
\begin{equation}
\text{BIC} = k \ln(N) - 2 \ln(\hat{L})
\end{equation}

At daily and weekly trading horizons ($D_1$ through $D_4$), the Student-$t$ copula wins the tournament. Tail dependence is modest ($\lambda_L \le 0.070$) and completely symmetric ($\text{TAR} = 0.000$). 

However, once we reach the quarterly business-cycle scale ($D_5$, 32 to 64 trading days), the copula structure shifts fundamentally. The asymmetric Gumbel copula wins decisively. Lower tail crash dependence jumps from $0.042$ to $0.318$, representing a \textbf{$657\%$ surge in joint crash probability}. Simultaneously, the Timescale Asymmetry Ratio jumps to $+0.286$, while upper tail dependence remains near zero ($\lambda_U = 0.032$). 

This provides clear empirical proof:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Financial assets crash together over extended holding periods, but recover on their own.}
\end{quote}
Assuming that cross-asset tail dependence is static across time horizons severely understates portfolio risk.

\subsection{Supervisory Hypothesis Testing}
We evaluate model performance through four established supervisory criteria:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Kupiec POF Likelihood Ratio Test:} Tests whether the observed breach count $x$ over $N$ observations matches the expected nominal rate ($H_0: p = 0.01$) \cite{kupiec1995}:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{LR}_{\text{POF}} = -2 \ln \left[ \frac{(1-p_0)^{N-x} p_0^x}{(1 - \hat{p})^{N-x} \hat{p}^x} \right] \sim \chi^2(1)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Christoffersen Independence Test:} Tests whether breaches cluster together in consecutive time windows \cite{christoffersen1998}:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{LR}_{\text{ind}} = -2 \ln \left[ \frac{L(\pi)}{L(\pi_{01}, \pi_{11})} \right] \sim \chi^2(1)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Basel Committee Traffic Light Zones:} Evaluates 250-day scaled breach counts at $99\%$ VaR:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Green Zone} ($\le 4$ breaches): Approved model ($k = 3.0$).
        \item \textbf{Yellow Zone} (5 to 9 breaches): Capital penalty surcharge ($k \in [3.40, 3.85]$).
        \item \textbf{Red Zone} ($\ge 10$ breaches): Model rejected ($k = 4.0$).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fissler-Ziegel (FZ) Strictly Consistent Loss:} Joint zero-homogeneous scoring function for $(\text{VaR}_{99\%}, \text{ES}_{97.5\%})$ \cite{fissler2016}:
    \begin{equation}
    S(v, e, y) = \frac{1}{e} \left[ v + \frac{(y - v) I_{\{y > v\}}}{1 - \alpha} \right] + \ln(e) - 1
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Actionable Policy: The H-TCM Multiplier}
\label{sec:htcm}

The competition mandates: \textit{``Give one concrete recommendation a risk manager could act on tomorrow.''}

\subsection{The Closed-Form Formula}
We introduce the \textbf{Horizon-Conditioned Tail Capital Multiplier (H-TCM)}, a practical formula that can be inserted directly into existing risk management software:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:htcm}
\boxed{
\text{VaR}_h^* = \text{VaR}_1 \times \sqrt{h} \times \left[ 1 + \kappa \cdot \max\left(0, \frac{\lambda_L(h) - \lambda_L(1)}{\lambda_L(1) + \epsilon}\right) \right]
}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\text{VaR}_1$: 1-day baseline VaR estimate.
    \item $\sqrt{h}$: Standard square-root-of-time factor.
    \item $\lambda_L(h)$: Lower tail dependence at scale $h$.
    \item $\lambda_L(1)$: 1-day baseline lower tail dependence ($D_1$).
    \item $\kappa$: Desk calibration factor ($\kappa = 0.35$).
    \item $\epsilon$: Numerical stabilizer ($10^{-6}$).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This research provides a direct answer to the competition challenge: \textbf{Tail dependence changes substantially with the investment horizon}. In our five-asset portfolio, lower tail crash dependence increases by over $650\%$ from daily noise scales ($0.042$) to quarterly horizons ($0.318$) because investors liquidate Treasuries and Gold to cover margin calls during major drawdowns. Ignoring this behavior causes traditional square-root scaling to underestimate multi-day portfolio risk. By adopting the QuantEdge multiscale wavelet-copula methodology and the closed-form H-TCM multiplier, risk desks can accurately measure multi-horizon risk and maintain resilient capital reserves across all market environments.
