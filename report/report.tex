\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Most banks and asset managers scale daily Value-at-Risk (VaR) to multi-day horizons using the standard Basel square-root-of-time rule ($\text{VaR}_h = \text{VaR}_1 \times \sqrt{h}$). This rule assumes that asset co-movements during extreme events remain identical whether positions are held for an afternoon or for three months. In this paper, we directly answer the official SAIFA Quant Edge 1.0 research question: \textit{``Does tail dependence change with the investment horizon, and what does ignoring this do to a portfolio's measured risk?''} Using daily data from 2015 to 2026 across a five-pillar institutional universe (US Equities, Tech, Long Treasuries, Gold, and High-Yield Credit), we decompose returns across timescales using the Maximal Overlap Discrete Wavelet Transform (MODWT). After filtering out volatility clustering with AR(1)-GJR-GARCH(1,1) models and modeling fat tails using Extreme Value Theory (EVT-POT), we run an automated tournament across five copula families at every timescale.

Our empirical findings demonstrate that cross-asset tail crash dependence changes across investment horizons. Lower tail crash dependence is highest at short frequencies ($D_1 \approx 0.189, D_2 \approx 0.201, D_3 \approx 0.176$), but drops noticeably at longer monthly and macroeconomic scales ($D_4 \approx 0.098, D_5 \approx 0.060, S_5 \approx 0.099$). Crucially, benchmarking against a Gaussian copula with matched correlation reveals that high-frequency co-exceedance ($D_1$, 2 to 4 days, $\hat{\lambda}_L = 0.189$ vs Gaussian benchmark $0.185$) is largely accounted for by linear correlation (excess tail dependence of only $+0.004$), with positive excess observed at $D_1$ to $D_3$ (peaking at $D_2$, excess $+0.030$), while excess tail dependence turns slightly negative at longer scales ($D_4, D_5, S_5$, between $-0.011$ and $-0.022$). Across all scales, the Student-$t$ copula is selected as optimal by Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), indicating elliptical fat-tailed dependence. Over 875 out-of-sample trading days (2023 to 2026), conventional square-root-of-time scaling was statistically conservative at 5-day and 20-day horizons (0 to 3 breaches vs $\approx 8.7$ expected), while the 1-day Parametric Gaussian model generates 13 breaches (a $1.49\%$ failure rate vs $1.00\%$ nominal target, $p = 0.178$ under Kupiec POF). We introduce the \textbf{Horizon-Conditioned Tail Capital Multiplier (H-TCM)} as an actionable contingent macroprudential overlay that provides a targeted $+2.2\%$ precautionary capital buffer when horizon tail dependence spikes, guarding against crisis contagion while avoiding permanent capital drag during calm markets.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction \& Research Question}
\label{sec:intro}

In quantitative risk management, Value-at-Risk ($\text{VaR}_\alpha$) and Expected Shortfall ($\text{ES}_\alpha$) form the core metrics used to determine regulatory capital buffers under the Basel framework \cite{basel1996, mcneil2015}. Under Basel guidelines, banks calculate their 1-day or 10-day risk numbers. When trading desks manage illiquid assets or hold positions across weeks, they almost universally rely on the classic square-root-of-time rule:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:basel_sqrt}
\text{VaR}_h = \text{VaR}_1 \times \sqrt{h}
\end{equation}
This formula was popularized decades ago as a computational shortcut. Mathematically, it requires two strict assumptions: asset returns must be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) normal random variables, and the joint correlation structure between assets must remain completely unchanged across all holding periods.

In real financial markets, both assumptions fall apart:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single-Asset Margins Have Fat Tails:} Returns cluster in volatile bursts, react more violently to bad news than good news (the leverage effect), and produce extreme losses far more often than a bell curve predicts.
    \item \textbf{Joint Tail Dependence Changes with the Horizon:} High-frequency day traders, weekly swing traders, and quarterly pension rebalancers operate on completely different clocks \cite{gencay2001, percival2000}. Two assets that move independently over an afternoon can become heavily tied together over a multi-week horizon.
\end{enumerate}

This study directly resolves the central competition inquiry:
\begin{quote}
\textit{``Does tail dependence change with the investment horizon, and what does ignoring this do to a portfolio's measured risk?''}
\end{quote}

Our core contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We show why standard decimated wavelets (DWT) fail in empirical finance by discarding observations, and use the shift-invariant \textbf{Maximal Overlap Discrete Wavelet Transform (MODWT)} to keep all $N = 2,889$ observations across every decomposition level.
    \item We filter out volatility clustering with an $\text{AR}(1)\text{-GJR-GARCH}(1,1)$ model and fit Generalized Pareto Distributions ($\text{GPD}$) to the extreme $10\%$ tails, ensuring pure uniform margins before copula estimation.
    \item We run an automated tournament across five copula families (Gaussian, Student-$t$, Clayton, Gumbel, Frank) across six timescale bands, demonstrating that fat-tailed Student-$t$ co-dependence dominates across holding periods while empirical tail crash dependence remains persistent.
    \item We demonstrate through 875 days of out-of-sample backtesting (2023 to 2026) the performance of benchmark and multiscale risk estimators, and deliver the \textbf{Horizon-Conditioned Tail Capital Multiplier (H-TCM)} as an actionable contingent overlay for institutional desks.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Asset Universe \& Economic Intuition}
\label{sec:universe}

Many empirical studies examine pairs of tech stocks (such as Apple and Microsoft). While easy to model, these pairs provide little insight into portfolio risk because tech stocks share high positive beta across all horizons. To examine whether cross-asset diversification holds up under stress, we analyze a five-pillar institutional portfolio from January 2, 2015 to June 30, 2026 ($N = 2,889$ trading days):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SPY} ($30\%$): S\&P 500 ETF (Core US equity market).
    \item \textbf{QQQ} ($20\%$): Invesco QQQ Trust (High-beta growth and technology).
    \item \textbf{TLT} ($25\%$): 20+ Year Treasury Bond ETF (Duration hedge and flight to safety).
    \item \textbf{GLD} ($15\%$): SPDR Gold Shares (Inflation hedge and alternative store of value).
    \item \textbf{HYG} ($10\%$): iShares High Yield Corporate Bond ETF (Credit spread and liquidity risk).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cross-Asset Diversification Across Timescales}
On an ordinary trading day ($D_1$: 2 to 4 days), Treasuries (TLT) and Gold (GLD) serve as effective hedges against stock market drops. When equities decline slightly, investors buy bonds or gold, keeping linear correlation low or negative. This is the foundation of the traditional balanced portfolio.

However, during market shocks, liquidity demands can alter cross-asset dynamics. Leveraged desks face margin calls on losing equity and credit positions and may liquidate liquid holdings (Treasuries and Gold) to meet liquidity calls. Examining a diversified portfolio across scales reveals whether cross-asset tail dependence shifts from high-frequency noise bands to quarterly macroeconomic cycles.

\section{Multiscale Wavelet-Copula Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{MODWT Wavelet Filter Bank}
The standard Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) downsamples the series by a factor of two at each octave ($N \to N/2 \to N/4$). By scale $J=5$, only $N/32$ data points remain (less than 90 observations from a 2,889-day history). Fitting Extreme Value Theory tails or copulas on 90 points is statistically unreliable.

To preserve full statistical power, we use the \textbf{Maximal Overlap Discrete Wavelet Transform (MODWT)} \cite{percival2000}. MODWT is shift-invariant and preserves the complete sample length $N$ at every octave. We select the Symlet filter bank ($\text{sym8}$, filter length $L=16$) because it provides near-linear phase and minimizes edge distortions. The high-pass filter $\tilde{h}$ and low-pass scaling filter $\tilde{g}$ are rescaled by $1/\sqrt{2}$:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{h}_l = \frac{h_l}{\sqrt{2}}, \quad \tilde{g}_l = \frac{g_l}{\sqrt{2}}
\end{equation}
At scale $j \in \{1, \dots, J\}$, filter steps are dilated by $2^{j-1}$. Wavelet detail coefficients $\widetilde{W}_j$ and scaling approximation coefficients $\widetilde{V}_j$ are calculated via circular convolution:
\begin{align}
\widetilde{W}_j[t] &= \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} \tilde{h}_l \widetilde{V}_{j-1}[(t - 2^{j-1} l) \bmod N] \\
\widetilde{V}_j[t] &= \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} \tilde{g}_l \widetilde{V}_{j-1}[(t - 2^{j-1} l) \bmod N]
\end{align}
where $\widetilde{V}_0 = R_t$ represents the raw daily log return series.

\subsection{Additive Multiresolution Analysis (MRA)}
Through inverse pyramid filtering, MODWT provides an exact additive multiresolution reconstruction:
\begin{equation}
R_t = \sum_{j=1}^J D_{j,t} + S_{J,t}
\end{equation}
Here $D_j$ represents scale-specific detail fluctuations and $S_J$ captures the smooth macroeconomic trend. In our data pipeline, we verify that this decomposition reconstructs original returns with machine precision:
\begin{equation}
\max_{t} \left| R_t - \left( \sum_{j=1}^5 D_{j,t} + S_{5,t} \right) \right| = 3.77 \times 10^{-14} \ll 10^{-10}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textit{Boundary effects and horizon semantics:} MODWT circular convolution introduces boundary filter overlap: for filter length $L=16$ at decomposition level $J=5$, the equivalent filter width is $L_5 = (2^5 - 1)(L - 1) + 1 = 466$ days, affecting approximately $23\%$ of observations at the slowest scales ($D_5, S_5$). Furthermore, an econometric wavelet frequency band (e.g., $D_4$, isolating 16--32 day oscillations) differs conceptually from a cumulative $h$-day holding period return, which inherently integrates all fluctuations up to horizon $h$.

\subsection{Semi-Parametric GARCH-EVT Margins}
Fitting static copulas directly on raw returns is econometrically invalid because financial returns violate the standard independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) assumption due to volatility clustering \cite{mcneil2015}. A cluster of volatile days can easily be mistaken for structural tail dependence when it is simply a shared volatility spike.

We solve this using a two-stage semi-parametric filter:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Volatility Filtering:} For each asset detail series $D_j$, we fit an $\text{AR}(1)\text{-GJR-GARCH}(1,1)$ model with Student-$t$ innovations \cite{gjr1993}:
    \begin{align}
    r_t &= \mu + \phi r_{t-1} + \epsilon_t, \quad \epsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t \\
    \sigma_t^2 &= \omega + (\alpha + \gamma I_{\{\epsilon_{t-1} < 0\}}) \epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2
    \end{align}
    where the parameter $\gamma$ captures the leverage effect (bad news generating higher volatility than good news).
    \item \textbf{EVT Peaks-Over-Threshold (POT):} Standardized filtered residuals $z_t = \epsilon_t / \sigma_t$ are modeled using the empirical cumulative distribution function (CDF) for the central body ($10\% \le u \le 90\%$) and Generalized Pareto Distributions ($\text{GPD}$) \cite{pickands1975} for the extreme $10\%$ tails:
    \begin{equation}
    F(z) = \begin{cases}
    q_L \left( 1 + \xi_L \frac{u_L - z}{\beta_L} \right)^{-1/\xi_L}, & z < u_L \\
    F_{\text{emp}}(z), & u_L \le z \le u_U \\
    1 - (1 - q_U) \left( 1 + \xi_U \frac{z - u_U}{\beta_U} \right)^{-1/\xi_U}, & z > u_U
    \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Probability Integral Transform (PIT):} Transformed margins $U_i = F_i(z_i)$ are verified as independent $\text{Uniform}(0, 1)$ random variables via empirical quantile checks ($\approx 5.0\%$ in the bottom $5\%$ tail). We note as a methodological caveat that in-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov $p$-values on probability integral transforms are uniformly $1.000$ because the semi-parametric empirical CDF is calibrated on the identical historical sample. Generalized Pareto shape parameters $\xi$ on extreme tails are numerically constrained within $[-0.5, 0.8]$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scale-Optimal Copula Tournament}
According to Sklar's Theorem \cite{sklar1959, embrechts2002}, any multivariate distribution $F(x_1, \dots, x_d)$ decomposes uniquely into its marginal CDFs and a copula function $C$:
\begin{equation}
F(x_1, \dots, x_d) = C(F_1(x_1), \dots, F_d(x_d))
\end{equation}
At each scale $D_1$ through $D_5$ and $S_5$, we run an automated tournament across five copula families fitted via Maximum Likelihood Estimation:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gaussian Copula:} Zero tail dependence benchmark ($\lambda_L = \lambda_U = 0$).
    \item \textbf{Student-$t$ Copula:} Symmetric tail dependence:
    \begin{equation}
    \lambda_L = \lambda_U = 2 t_{\nu + 1}\left( -\sqrt{\frac{(\nu + 1)(1 - \rho)}{1 + \rho}} \right) > 0
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Clayton Copula:} Asymmetric lower crash dependence ($\lambda_L = 2^{-1/\theta} > 0, \lambda_U = 0$).
    \item \textbf{Gumbel Copula:} Asymmetric upper-tail dependence ($\lambda_U = 2 - 2^{1/\theta} > 0, \lambda_L = 0$).
    \item \textbf{Frank Copula:} Radial symmetry with zero tail dependence ($\lambda_L = \lambda_U = 0$).
To ensure computational tractability across five assets ($d=5$), candidate Archimedean copulas (Clayton, Gumbel, Frank) are estimated via pairwise composite log-likelihood (PCL) summation across all $\binom{5}{2} = 10$ pairs \cite{mcneil2015}, while elliptical candidates (Gaussian and Student-$t$) evaluate full 5-dimensional joint likelihoods. Consequently, candidate Archimedean BICs are based on composite pairwise likelihoods and are not directly commensurate with 5D joint likelihoods. Between Gaussian and Student-$t$ (which share the exact same full 5-dimensional joint likelihood construction and parameter dimensionality), the Student-$t$ copula strictly dominates across every timescale ($\Delta \text{BIC} \approx -182$ at $D_1$, $-145$ at $D_2$, $-118$ at $D_3$, $-36$ at $D_4$, $-68$ at $D_5$, $-134$ at $S_5$), demonstrating the empirical superiority of elliptical fat-tailed dependence over Gaussian co-movement. The winning copula per timescale is selected by minimizing the Bayesian Information Criterion:
\begin{equation}
\text{BIC} = k \ln(N) - 2 \ln(\hat{L})
\end{equation}

\subsection{Multiscale Risk Overlay vs. Signal Inversion}
Rather than attempting synthetic frequency-domain time-series inversion (which introduces phase distortion and cross-scale dependence artifacts), our proposed framework operates as a \textbf{Multiscale Tail-Adjusted Copula Overlay}: it couples the full-spectrum EVT-Student-$t$ copula with horizon-specific tail-dependence multipliers derived from the MODWT wavelet tournament. This conditions portfolio capital requirements on empirically detected tail clustering at target horizons.

Across all decomposed timescales ($D_1$ through $S_5$), the Student-$t$ copula wins the tournament by Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), indicating that multiscale co-dependence is well-modeled by elliptical fat-tailed dynamics rather than single-parameter Archimedean structures. The empirical Timescale Asymmetry Ratio remains tightly bounded around zero ($\text{TAR} \in [-0.013, +0.011]$).

\textbf{Disentangling Tail Dependence from Linear Correlation:} A critical methodological pitfall in empirical risk modeling is mistaking linear correlation for genuine non-linear tail dependence. At a $5\%$ quantile threshold ($q = 0.05$), two assets governed by a plain Gaussian copula with zero asymptotic tail dependence will nonetheless produce positive joint co-exceedance purely due to linear correlation $\rho$:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\text{Gauss}}(q; \rho) = \frac{1}{q} \Phi_2\left( \Phi^{-1}(q), \Phi^{-1}(q); \rho \right)
\end{equation}
To benchmark whether tail co-movement reflects true tail crash dependence or background linear correlation, we compute the exact bivariate Gaussian benchmark $\lambda_{\text{Gauss}}(0.05; \rho_{ij})$ for each asset pair and report the \textbf{Excess Tail Dependence} ($\text{Excess} = \hat{\lambda}_L - \bar{\lambda}_{\text{Gauss}}$) in Table \ref{tab:copula_tournament}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Microstructure Scale ($D_1$, 2 to 4d):} Empirical co-exceedance is $\hat{\lambda}_L = 0.189$, but the Gaussian benchmark with matched linear correlation is $0.185$. The excess tail dependence is negligible ($+0.004$), consistent with high-frequency tail co-movement being driven almost entirely by linear correlation rather than non-linear crash dependence.
    \item \textbf{Weekly Swing Scale ($D_2$, 4 to 8d):} Empirical tail dependence reaches its point estimate peak at $\hat{\lambda}_L = 0.201$ against a Gaussian benchmark of $0.171$, generating an \textbf{Excess Tail Dependence} of $+0.030$. A moving-block bootstrap (block size $b=64$ days, 300 replications) produces a $95\%$ confidence interval of $[0.154, 0.233]$, which overlaps with the $D_1$ interval $[0.143, 0.239]$. This demonstrates that the $0.012$ gap between $D_1$ and $D_2$ lies within finite-sample estimation uncertainty; the observation is consistent with, but does not prove, non-linear crash clustering at weekly frequencies.
    \item \textbf{Intermediate Cycles ($D_4, D_5$, 16 to 64d):} Co-exceedance drops substantially to $\hat{\lambda}_L = 0.098$ ($D_4$) and $0.060$ ($D_5$), both falling below their Gaussian benchmarks ($0.109$ and $0.074$, excess $-0.011$ and $-0.014$), indicating that mid-horizon diversification is less impaired by non-linear crash dependence.
    \item \textbf{Secular Macro Trend ($S_5$, $>64$d):} Co-exceedance remains subdued at $\hat{\lambda}_L = 0.099$ ($95\%$ CI $[0.026, 0.162]$) against a Gaussian benchmark of $0.122$ (excess $-0.022$). In this reference environment, the data do not support an upward rebound at macro horizons, showing that extreme crash clustering attenuates at longer horizons.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Supervisory Hypothesis Testing}
We evaluate model performance through four established supervisory criteria:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Kupiec POF Likelihood Ratio Test:} Tests whether the observed breach count $x$ over $N$ observations matches the expected nominal rate ($H_0: p = 0.01$) \cite{kupiec1995}:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{LR}_{\text{POF}} = -2 \ln \left[ \frac{(1-p_0)^{N-x} p_0^x}{(1 - \hat{p})^{N-x} \hat{p}^x} \right] \sim \chi^2(1)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Christoffersen Independence Test:} Tests whether breaches cluster together in consecutive time windows \cite{christoffersen1998}:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{LR}_{\text{ind}} = -2 \ln \left[ \frac{L(\pi)}{L(\pi_{01}, \pi_{11})} \right] \sim \chi^2(1)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Basel Committee Traffic Light Zones:} Evaluates 250-day scaled breach counts at $99\%$ VaR:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Green Zone} ($\le 4$ breaches): Approved model ($k = 3.0$).
        \item \textbf{Yellow Zone} (5 to 9 breaches): Capital penalty surcharge ($k \in [3.40, 3.85]$).
        \item \textbf{Red Zone} ($\ge 10$ breaches): Model rejected ($k = 4.0$).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fissler-Ziegel (FZ) Strictly Consistent Loss:} Joint zero-homogeneous scoring function for $(\text{VaR}_{99\%}, \text{ES}_{99\%})$ \cite{fissler2016}:
    \begin{equation}
    S(v, e, y) = \frac{1}{e} \left[ v + \frac{(y - v) I_{\{y > v\}}}{1 - \alpha} \right] + \ln(e) - 1
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Statistical Diagnostic Context for Overlapping Windows}
Multi-day holding period returns are computed via overlapping rolling windows ($h=5$ and $h=20$). Consequently, adjacent observations share $h-1$ days of common return realizations, inducing moving-average serial autocorrelation. While standard in institutional desktop benchmarking, overlapping windows violate the independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) assumption underlying the Christoffersen Markov test, producing potential test size distortions. Accordingly, multi-day backtest metrics are presented strictly as descriptive breach diagnostics rather than exact formal regulatory approval tests.

\section{Actionable Policy: The H-TCM Multiplier}
\label{sec:htcm}

The competition mandates: \textit{``Give one concrete recommendation a risk manager could act on tomorrow.''}

\subsection{The Closed-Form Formula}
We introduce the \textbf{Horizon-Conditioned Tail Capital Multiplier (H-TCM)}, a practical formula that can be inserted directly into existing risk management software:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:htcm}
\boxed{
\text{VaR}_h^* = \text{VaR}_1 \times \sqrt{h} \times \left[ 1 + \kappa \cdot \max\left(0, \frac{\hat{\lambda}_L(h) - \hat{\lambda}_L(1)}{\hat{\lambda}_L(1) + \epsilon}\right) \right]
}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\text{VaR}_1$: 1-day baseline VaR estimate.
    \item $\sqrt{h}$: Standard square-root-of-time factor.
    \item $\hat{\lambda}_L(h)$: Empirical lower tail dependence at scale $h$.
    \item $\hat{\lambda}_L(1)$: 1-day baseline lower tail dependence ($D_1$).
    \item $\kappa$: Desk calibration factor ($\kappa = 0.35$).
    \item $\epsilon$: Numerical stabilizer ($10^{-6}$).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Empirical Evaluation and Candid Limitations:} 
We provide a transparent evaluation of the H-TCM recommendation:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Precautionary Buffer vs. Statistical Noise:} The $+2.2\%$ capital buffer at the 5-day horizon stems from an empirical difference of $\hat{\lambda}_L(D_2) - \hat{\lambda}_L(D_1) = 0.201 - 0.189 = 0.012$. In finite samples without asymptotic hypothesis testing, a difference of $0.012$ lies within sampling variation. The overlay functions as an asymmetric precautionary risk buffer rather than a statistically proven divergence.
    \item \textbf{Performance in Calm Regimes:} In the 2023--2026 backtest, standard square-root scaling was already conservative (3 breaches at 5d). Applying the $+2.2\%$ H-TCM surcharge yielded the exact same 3 breaches while locking up additional capital, resulting in a slightly higher (worse) Fissler-Ziegel score ($-2.9599$ vs $-2.9744$) due to the scoring penalty on excess capital lockup.
    \item \textbf{Collapse at Long Horizons:} At 20-day and 40-day horizons, because scale tail dependence falls below the 1-day baseline, the floored multiplier $\max(0, \dots)$ is exactly zero, making the multiplier $1.000$ (identical to standard scaling).
    \item \textbf{Untested Crisis Performance:} H-TCM is intended as a contingent macroprudential safety rail for systemic liquidity squeezes (such as 2008 or March 2020), where multiscale tail dependence surges simultaneously across horizons. It remains untested out-of-sample in a severe historical crisis.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This research provides a direct and empirical resolution to the official competition inquiry:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Does tail dependence change with the investment horizon?} 
    Yes. Across our five-asset universe, the Student-$t$ copula is selected as optimal across all timescale bands, indicating that multi-asset joint extremes are best captured by elliptical fat-tailed dependence rather than Archimedean asymmetry. However, empirical co-exceedance over the Gaussian benchmark is modestly positive at short horizons ($D_1$ to $D_3$, peaking at $D_2$ with $+0.030$, though overlapping bootstrap intervals show this peak is within estimation noise), while at longer holding periods ($D_4, D_5, S_5$), empirical co-exceedance attenuates substantially ($0.060$ to $0.099$) with slightly negative excess relative to Gaussian benchmarks.
    \item \textbf{What does ignoring this do to a portfolio's measured risk?} 
    At short horizons (1-day), ignoring fat tails and tail dependence (Parametric Gaussian) understates measured risk, producing 13 breaches ($1.49\%$ failure rate) compared to 5 breaches for copula-based specifications. However, at multi-day horizons (5-day, 20-day), conventional square-root-of-time scaling does not understate risk in normal trending markets; it was statistically conservative (0 to 3 breaches observed). The H-TCM multiplier provides an actionable, contingent macroprudential overlay to guard against multiscale tail contagion during stressed regimes without imposing unnecessary capital drag in quiet markets.
\end{enumerate}
